Snakes, Dragons & Ladders is the game most of us learned before we could read the numbers on the board. Our version keeps that simplicity, adds a 3D-styled board with crocodiles and a fire dragon, and lets up to four people race from anywhere in the world. This guide walks through everything you need to know, from the first roll to the exact landing on 100, and explains how the online options and the four AI opponents change the feel of a race.

## What you need

Nothing but a browser. The game runs on phones, tablets and desktops, and a race takes about ten minutes. You can play:

- **Solo against the computer**, with one to three AI opponents.
- **Online with strangers** through matchmaking (two to four players).
- **In a private room** with friends and family, with voice and text chat.

There is no setup to speak of: pick your mode, choose a pawn colour, and the board appears.

## Setup and the board

The board is a 10×10 grid of 100 squares. Square 1 is in the bottom-left corner and 100 is in the top-left. The numbering runs back and forth like a plough turning at the end of each furrow: the first row reads left to right from 1 to 10, the second reads right to left from 11 to 20, and so on. This zigzag pattern is called **boustrophedon** numbering, and it is why the path to the top looks like a snake itself.

Scattered over the board are ladders and three kinds of hazards: snakes, crocodiles and the fire dragon. Mechanically they all behave the same way; the dragon is simply the longest and most dramatic slide on the board.

Every pawn starts off the board, on an imaginary square 0. Players roll to decide who goes first, then take turns clockwise.

## How a turn works

1. Roll the single six-sided die.
2. Move your pawn forward exactly that many squares, following the numbering.
3. If you finish your move on a special square, resolve it immediately (climb or slide).
4. If you rolled a 6, roll again. Otherwise your turn ends.

That is the whole loop. There are no decisions to make during a move, which is exactly why the game works so well with young children and mixed-age groups: everyone has the same chance, and nobody needs a strategy briefing.

## All the rules

### Movement

You always move forward by the full number rolled. Two pawns may share a square; there is no capturing or sending opponents home.

### Ladders

Land exactly at the **foot** of a ladder and you climb immediately to its **top**. Passing over the foot of a ladder does nothing; only landing on it counts. If the top of the ladder happens to be the head of a snake (rare, but it happens on some layouts), you slide straight back down.

### Snakes, crocodiles and the dragon

Land on the **head** of a snake, croc or dragon and you slide to its **tail** at once. The dragon on our board is the biggest drop, so the squares around its head are the ones to dread in the late game. Again, only an exact landing triggers the slide.

### Bonus rolls for a 6

Rolling a 6 earns you another roll. Keep rolling while you keep throwing sixes, with one catch: **three 6s in a row forfeits the turn**. Your pawn goes back to where it stood before the first of the three sixes, and play passes to the next player. Think of it as the board's way of punishing greed.

### Finishing on exactly 100

You must land **exactly** on square 100 to win. If you are on 97 and roll a 5, you have overshot, so your pawn stays put and your turn ends. Only a 3 finishes the race from 97; a 1 or 2 inches you closer. This rule produces the nail-biting finishes the game is famous for, where a player sits two squares from victory for four turns while everyone else catches up.

### Ending the game

The first player to reach 100 wins. In a four-player game the remaining players can keep rolling for second and third place, or simply start a rematch; our rooms offer a rematch button so nobody has to re-queue.

## Strategy tips (such as they are)

Let's be honest: this is a game of pure luck. There is no move choice, so there is no strategy in the usual sense. What you *can* do is make the race more fun:

- **Call your shot.** Before you roll, say which square you are hoping for. It costs nothing and turns every roll into a tiny event.
- **Learn the board.** After a few games you will know which squares hide the dragon's head. Groaning in advance is half the entertainment.
- **Play best of three.** Single races can end in five minutes; a short series evens out the luck and gives the person who got eaten by a crocodile on turn two a chance to recover.

## Common mistakes

- **Counting the starting square.** When you move, the square you are standing on is square zero. Rolling a 4 from square 10 lands you on 14, not 13.
- **Climbing from the middle of a ladder.** Ladders only work from their foot. Likewise, slides only happen from the head of the snake.
- **Forgetting the exact-finish rule.** Overshooting 100 does not bounce you back; you simply do not move.
- **Rolling a fourth time.** After three sixes your turn is over and your pawn returns to its pre-sixes square. The game enforces this for you, but it helps to know why your pawn jumped backwards.

## Playing with friends and family

This is the game we recommend for a first online family night. Create a free account, add relatives on the Friends page, then **create a private room** from the game's mode selector and ping them to join. Private rooms hold up to four players, and voice chat means grandparents can commentate without touching a keyboard. If you would rather race strangers, pick **Online Multiplayer** and matchmaking fills the room; the race starts as soon as four players join, or after 60 seconds once at least two are ready.

Fewer than four humans? Fill the empty seats with AI players so the board is busy. Three-year-olds can take their turn by tapping the die, and the game won't let anyone move the wrong pawn.

## Choosing an AI level

![The four AI difficulty levels, from Buddy to Oracle](/blog/ai-levels.svg)

The difficulty picker offers Buddy, Nova, Titan and the Oracle. In games with real decisions these levels change how hard the computer thinks. In Snakes, Dragons & Ladders there are no decisions, so the AI cannot roll better dice than you; the random number generator is the same for every seat. In practice the levels mostly change pacing and personality: Buddy takes its turns quickly and cheerfully, while the Oracle pauses dramatically before each roll. Pick whichever feels right for the table, and do not expect the Oracle to win more often than Buddy here. If you want a game where the levels truly bite, try [Connect 4](/games/connect-4) or [Chess](/games/chess) next.

## Quick reference

| Situation | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| Roll 1–5 | Move that many squares; turn ends |
| Roll 6 | Move 6, then roll again |
| Three 6s in a row | Turn forfeited; pawn returns to its square before the sixes |
| Land on a ladder's foot | Climb to the top immediately |
| Land on a snake, croc or dragon head | Slide to the tail immediately |
| Pass over a ladder or head | Nothing happens |
| Roll more than needed for 100 | Stay put; turn ends |
| Land exactly on 100 | You win |
