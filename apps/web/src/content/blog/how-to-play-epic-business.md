Epic Business is our property-trading game for two to four players: buy districts, complete colour groups, build offices and towers, and collect rent until your rivals run out of credits. A full game takes about 45 minutes, so it is the centrepiece of a game night rather than a filler. This guide covers every rule, explains the parts that trip up new players (auctions, mortgages and building limits), and describes what to expect from the four AI opponents.

## What you need

A browser and either some friends or a few AI rivals. Epic Business supports online matchmaking, private rooms with voice chat, and any mix of humans and computer players. Because a game is long, we suggest a private room with people you know for your first few sessions.

## Setup

Every player starts with **1,500 credits** and a pawn on **Payday**, the corner square where the lap begins. The board is a ring of **40 spaces**: 22 districts arranged in eight colour groups, four stations, two utilities, card spaces for Fortune and Market, a couple of tax spaces and the four corners (Payday, Free Rest, Lock-up and Go to Lock-up). The game shuffles the Fortune and Market decks and picks a starting player at random.

## How a turn works

1. **Roll two dice** and move your pawn clockwise by the total.
2. **Resolve the space** you land on: buy or auction an unowned property, pay rent on an owned one, draw a card, pay tax, or go to Lock-up.
3. **Build, mortgage or trade** if you want to. You can do these at any point during your own turn.
4. If you rolled **doubles**, take another turn. **Three doubles in a row** sends you straight to Lock-up without passing Payday.

## All the rules

### Buying property

When you land on an unowned district, station or utility you may buy it for the listed price. If you decline, or cannot afford it, the property goes to **auction**: every player, including the one who passed, bids and the highest bid takes it at that price. Auctions are where bargains happen, so pay attention even when it is not your turn.

### Rent

Land on a property owned by someone else and you pay rent to them. The base rent on a bare district is modest. Rent **doubles when the owner holds the whole colour group**, and then climbs steeply with every office they build. Stations pay more the more stations their owner holds; utilities charge a multiple of your dice roll. You pay nothing on a mortgaged property. Owners in Lock-up still collect rent, so property is never a liability.

### Building offices and towers

You may build only on a colour group you own **completely**. Each district in the group can hold up to **four offices**, and a district with four offices can upgrade to a **tower**, which replaces the offices and charges the top rent. You must build evenly: you cannot put a second office on one district until every district in the group has one. Sell buildings back at half price when you need cash.

### Fortune and Market cards

Land on a card space and the top card of that deck resolves immediately. Fortune cards tend to be about you: move to a space, collect a windfall, pay a fee, go to Lock-up, or get out of Lock-up free. Market cards tend to be about the economy: everyone pays you, you pay everyone, repairs on your buildings, or a bonus for passing Payday. Card texts are examples rather than a fixed list; the game shows you exactly what each card does when it is drawn.

### Lock-up

You go to Lock-up by landing on Go to Lock-up, drawing a card that sends you there, or rolling three doubles in a row. While in Lock-up you still collect rent and may still trade and build. To get out, roll doubles on one of your next three turns, pay the release fee, or play a Get out of Lock-up card. After three failed attempts you pay the fee and move by your roll.

### Payday and mortgages

Each time you pass or land on Payday you collect **200 credits**. When you need cash, **mortgage** a property: the bank pays you half its price, it earns no rent until you repay the mortgage plus interest, and you cannot build on its colour group while any district in that group is mortgaged.

### Bankruptcy and winning

If you owe more than you can raise by selling buildings and mortgaging, you are bankrupt and leave the game; your assets go to the creditor (or back to the bank if you owed the bank). The **last player who has not gone bankrupt wins**. In a long four-player game it is fine to agree a time limit and count net worth instead.

## Trading

You may propose a trade with any player on your turn: properties, credits and Get out of Lock-up cards in any combination. Trades are how colour groups get completed, so expect the whole table to pay attention when one is proposed. A fair trade usually gives the other side something they need too, otherwise they have no reason to accept.

## Common mistakes

- **Passing on cheap property early.** Districts in the first stretch after Payday look unimpressive but are easy to complete and cheap to build on.
- **Building before you have a cash buffer.** Three offices on a busy group is wonderful until you land on someone's tower with 40 credits in hand.
- **Ignoring auctions.** The player who declines a property can still win it at auction, often for less than list price.
- **Letting mortgages linger.** A mortgaged district earns nothing and blocks building on its group.

## Playing with friends and family

Epic Business shines with people you can negotiate with. Set up a **private room**, switch on voice chat and let the haggling begin. With younger players, agree in advance that trades must be "fair enough" so nobody is talked out of their best property. If you cannot gather four people, add one or two AI seats; mixed tables work well because the AI never sulks about a lost auction.

## Choosing an AI level

![The four AI difficulty levels, from Buddy to Oracle](/blog/ai-levels.svg)

The AI personas differ in how often they play a deliberately random move rather than their best one, which produces distinct personalities over a 45-minute game:

- **Buddy** tends to buy almost anything it lands on, overpay at auction and forget to build. Great for a first game or for children.
- **Nova** plays a reasonable game but still makes enough odd decisions to feel human.
- **Titan** completes groups quickly and builds aggressively; expect it to pressure you around the board.
- **Oracle** tends to trade ruthlessly and only when the maths favours it, keeps a cash buffer, and builds to the sweet spot of three offices. Beating it is a genuine achievement.
