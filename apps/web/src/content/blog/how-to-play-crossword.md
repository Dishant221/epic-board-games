A crossword is a conversation with its setter. Every clue is a small promise: "the answer is this long, it means roughly this, and the letters you earn elsewhere will confirm it." Once you learn to read those promises, a grid that looked impossible becomes a chain of small, satisfying wins. This guide covers how our hand-made crosswords work on screen, how the numbering and clues fit together, and how to lean on the hint system without spoiling the fun.

## What you need

Just the game and a few spare minutes. Our Crossword is a solo puzzle that runs on phones, tablets and desktops. Each puzzle is written by hand, has its own colour theme, and reveals letters with a short animation when you get them right. There is no timer unless you want one, so set your own pace.

## Setup

Open the game and choose a puzzle. The grid appears on the left or top (depending on your screen) and the clue lists for **Across** and **Down** beside or beneath it. White squares take letters; black squares separate the words.

## How numbering works

Numbers live in the top-left corner of certain white squares. A square gets a number if it is the first letter of an answer, reading left to right (an across entry) or top to bottom (a down entry). Numbers are assigned in reading order, so you count along the top row, then the next row, and so on.

The important consequence: one square can start *both* an across and a down answer, and it uses a single number for both. That is why clue lists say "1 Across" and "1 Down". A square that starts only a down answer gets the next number but has no across clue, and vice versa. The number in brackets after each clue tells you how many letters the answer has.

## All the rules

- Fill the grid using the across and down clues. Every white square must hold a letter.
- Tap a square to select it. The clue for that square's word is highlighted so you know which one you are solving.
- Tap the selected square again to switch between its across word and its down word.
- Type letters to fill the word. The cursor moves on to the next square automatically.
- The puzzle checks your answers as you go, so a correct word is confirmed as soon as it is complete.
- If you are stuck, use a hint to reveal a single letter or the whole word.
- Fill every square correctly to finish the puzzle and trigger the completion animation.

## Reading the clues

Crossword clues come in a handful of flavours. Knowing the conventions turns "no idea" into "I can work with that".

- **Plain definitions.** "Large feline (4)" is LION. The clue is a synonym or description of the answer.
- **Fill in the blank.** "Once ___ a time (4)" is UPON. These are usually the easiest clues in the grid, so hunt for them first.
- **Abbreviations signal abbreviations.** If the clue contains an abbreviation such as "Dept." or "org.", the answer is probably abbreviated too.
- **Question marks mean wordplay.** "Flower of London? (6)" is THAMES — a river that flows. Expect a pun or a misdirection.
- **Plurals and tenses match.** A clue ending in "s" wants a plural answer; a clue in the past tense wants a past-tense answer. This alone often gives you the last letter.
- **Capitalisation hints at names.** A capital letter mid-clue usually points to a proper noun.

## A sensible solving order

1. **Read every clue once** without filling anything. You will spot two or three you know instantly.
2. **Fill-in-the-blank and short answers first.** Three- and four-letter words are limited; the letters they give you are gold.
3. **Work the crossings.** Each letter you place is also a letter in a perpendicular word. Switch direction (tap the square again) and see whether the crossing clue now makes sense.
4. **Return to the long answers** once a few letters are in place. "_ _ A _ E _ O _ K" is far friendlier than nine blanks.
5. **Finish the stubborn corners** with hints if you need them.

## Using hints well

Our hints come in two sizes. A **letter hint** fills one square in the selected word; a **word hint** completes the whole answer. Reach for the letter hint first, and reach for it in the square you think is most informative, usually the first letter of a long answer or a square that crosses another word you are stuck on. One letter often cracks two clues at once. Save the word hint for a corner where you have genuinely run out of ideas; even then, read the completed answer against its clue so you learn the convention that defeated you.

Because the puzzle checks answers as you type, you will know immediately when a word is right. If a word stays unconfirmed, do not assume the whole thing is wrong; check the crossing letters one by one.

## Common mistakes

- **Guessing a long word with no crossings.** Wait for two or three letters first.
- **Ignoring the letter count.** The number in brackets rules out most wrong answers before you type a thing.
- **Forgetting to switch direction.** If letters are landing in the wrong squares, tap the selected square to flip between across and down.
- **Spending all your hints in one corner.** Spread them out; a letter in a fresh area often unlocks more.

## Playing with friends and family

Crosswords are a quietly social game. Share one screen and give each person a clue type: one solves the fill-in-the-blanks, one hunts the proper nouns, one handles the wordplay. Or play "shout it out": read a clue aloud and let the first correct answer claim it. For a two-device variant, both players load the same puzzle and race, with hints either banned or capped at three each. Younger solvers enjoy the colour themes and the animated reveal, so let them do the typing while you read the clues.

## A little history

The first crossword as we know it was published by Arthur Wynne in the New York World on 21 December 1913. He called it a "Word-Cross", and a typesetting slip soon turned the name into "Cross-Word". The diamond-shaped grid had no black squares and clues such as "What bargain hunters enjoy". Within a decade crosswords were a craze on both sides of the Atlantic, and the cryptic variety that British solvers love grew out of the same roots in the 1920s and 1930s.
