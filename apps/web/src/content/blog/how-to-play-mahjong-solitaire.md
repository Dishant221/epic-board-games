Mahjong solitaire borrows the beautiful tiles of the four-player Chinese game and turns them into a single-player matching puzzle. The rules are simple enough to learn in a minute, but clearing a full layout without getting stuck takes a little planning, and that planning is where the pleasure is. This guide explains the rules used on Epic Board Games, what all those tiles actually are, and the handful of habits that separate players who finish layouts from players who shuffle their way through them.

## What you need

Just you and a browser. Mahjong Solitaire here is a one-player game with a hint button that highlights a matching pair and a shuffle button for when no moves remain. Layouts load with a random tile arrangement each time, so no two games are the same.

## Setup

The game lays out 144 tiles in a stacked pattern, several tiles deep in places. The classic arrangement is the **turtle** (sometimes called the pyramid): a broad body of tiles with a raised centre, a head and tail sticking out at either end, and a single tile perched on top. Tap any tile to select it; tap a matching free tile to remove both. The counter shows how many tiles remain, and when the last pair disappears you win.

## How a turn works

There are no turns as such and no clock pressure unless you set yourself one. Look for two identical tiles that are both free, tap them, and they vanish. Repeat until the board is clear. If no legal pair exists you are stuck, and that is the moment the shuffle button is for, though a careful player rarely needs it.

## All the rules

### What makes a tile free

A tile can be removed only if **both** of these are true:

1. Nothing is resting on top of it, even partly.
2. At least one of its long sides (left or right) is completely open, with no tile touching it on that side.

A tile in the middle of a row with neighbours on both sides is blocked. A tile with a neighbour on only one side is free. A tile with nothing on either side is free. Tiles above or below (in the sense of the layout's rows) do not matter; only the two long sides and the layer above count.

### Matching

Tap two **identical** free tiles to remove them. Identical means the same suit and the same value: a 3 of bamboo matches another 3 of bamboo, not a 3 of dots.

There are two exceptions. **Any flower matches any flower** (there are four different flower tiles), and **any season matches any season** (four different season tiles). This is because the traditional set has only one of each flower and one of each season, so without the exception they could never be paired.

### Hints and shuffles

The hint button highlights one available matching pair. The shuffle button rearranges the remaining tiles in their current positions so new pairs become available. Both are always allowed, but see the strategy section on why you should ration them.

### Winning

Clear every tile from the board and you win. The game is lost only in the sense that you may need a shuffle to continue; the layout itself is never taken away from you.

## The tiles

The standard set of 144 tiles comes straight from traditional mahjong:

- **Three suits** numbered 1 to 9, four copies of each number: **bamboos** (the 1 of bamboo is often drawn as a bird), **characters** or "craks" (Chinese numerals with the character for ten thousand), and **dots** or "circles".
- **Four winds**: east, south, west and north, four copies of each.
- **Three dragons**: red (中), green (發) and white (a blank or framed tile), four copies of each.
- **Four flowers** (plum, orchid, chrysanthemum, bamboo) and **four seasons** (spring, summer, autumn, winter), one copy of each.

That gives 108 suit tiles, 16 winds, 12 dragons and 8 flowers and seasons. Knowing the set matters for strategy, because almost every tile has exactly four copies in the layout.

## Strategy tips

### Height before width

Tiles stacked on top of others are blocking everything beneath them. Removing the top tile of the turtle frees the layer below, which frees the layer below that. Removing an end tile from a long bottom row frees only one neighbour. When you have a choice, take the pair that is higher up.

### Long rows and the two ends

The long horizontal rows in the turtle's body can only be eaten from their ends. If one of the two end tiles has no free match anywhere, that whole row is stalled. Prioritise matches that open up long rows, and look at the tail and head tiles early, since they often block the whole left and right flanks.

### Count to four

Because every ordinary tile has four copies, when you see a free pair you have a decision: remove them now, or wait. If the other two copies are both buried, remove the free pair without hesitation; nothing is lost. If one of the other copies is also free, you have three free tiles of the same kind and the choice of which two to remove matters. Pick the two that unblock the most.

### When not to take a match

Sometimes the pair in front of you is harmless: both tiles sit on the open edge and block nothing. Leaving them gives you a guaranteed move later when you might otherwise be stuck. Spending a free pair that does not unlock anything is like spending a spare tempo in checkers; keep it for when you need it.

### Ration hints and shuffles

A hint shows you *a* pair, not the *best* pair, and taking whatever it points at often opens less than a move you would have found yourself. Use it when you have genuinely scanned the board twice. Shuffle only when the game tells you there are no moves; a shuffle with many tiles left usually produces a new layout that is harder, not easier, because the stacks stay where they are.

## Common mistakes

Clearing the easy edge tiles first and leaving a tall, locked centre. Breaking up a free pair by matching one of them with a third copy that was blocking nothing. Forgetting that a tile under another is blocked even if its sides look open. And hitting shuffle out of impatience rather than necessity.

## Playing with friends and family

Mahjong Solitaire is single-player on Epic Board Games, but it is a surprisingly social game on a shared screen or a voice call: one person scans the left half, one the right, and you argue about which pair unlocks more. It is also a gentle option for a family member who finds competitive games stressful, and the hint system means nobody is ever truly stuck. If you want head-to-head play, the same tile-matching instincts transfer well to [Epic Puzzle Board](/games/epic-puzzle), which supports two players.
