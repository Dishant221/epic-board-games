You do not need to memorise twenty moves of theory to play a good opening. You need a handful of principles and a few reliable set-ups you understand well enough to reach a sensible middlegame. This guide gives you both: the ideas first, then the concrete moves, then the one trap you must be able to see coming. All moves are in standard algebraic notation, the same coordinates our board prints in the margins.

## The five principles

### 1. Fight for the centre

The four squares d4, e4, d5 and e5 are the crossroads of the board. A piece in the centre reaches more squares than one on the edge, and pawns there cramp your opponent. The simplest way to stake a claim is to push a centre pawn two squares on move one.

### 2. Develop knights before bishops

Knights have an obvious best square early on (f3 and c3 for White, f6 and c6 for Black), while bishops are more flexible and can wait a move to see where they are most useful. Getting the knights out first also clears the way to castle.

### 3. Castle early

Your king is safest behind an unbroken wall of pawns in the corner, and castling also brings a rook towards the centre. Aim to castle within your first eight to ten moves unless something concrete stops you.

### 4. Move each piece once

Every time you shuffle a piece you have already developed, your opponent gets a free move to bring out a new one. Unless you are winning material or responding to a threat, develop something new instead.

### 5. Keep the queen home for now

The queen is your most valuable piece, which makes it the easiest to attack. Bring it out early and it will be chased around by knights and bishops that are developing with tempo. Let the minor pieces lead.

## Openings for White

### The Italian Game: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4

The most instructive opening to learn first. White claims the centre, develops a knight that attacks e5, then points a bishop at f7, the weakest square in Black's camp because only the king defends it. From here 4.d3, 5.O-O and 6.c3 give a solid, flexible position. Black's two main replies are 3...Bc5 (the Giuoco Piano, "quiet game") and 3...Nf6 (the Two Knights Defence), which invites the sharper 4.Ng5.

### The Ruy Lopez: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bb5

Same first two moves, but the bishop goes to b5 to put pressure on the knight that defends e5. The classical response is 3...a6, and after 4.Ba4 Nf6 5.O-O Be7 both sides have calm, logical development. It is the opening of champions for a reason, but the ideas are slower to show themselves than in the Italian, so learn it second.

### The Scotch Game: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.d4

White opens the centre at once. After 3...exd4 4.Nxd4 the position is open and pieces fly; it is a good choice if you prefer action to manoeuvring and want practice at calculating.

### The Queen's Gambit: 1.d4 d5 2.c4

White offers the c-pawn to pull Black's d-pawn off the centre. It is not really a gambit: if Black accepts with 2...dxc4 (the Queen's Gambit Accepted), White recovers the pawn with 3.e3 or 3.e4 and has a strong centre. Most players decline with 2...e6 (the Queen's Gambit Declined), after which 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Bg5 Be7 5.e3 O-O 6.Nf3 is the classical main line.

### The London System: 1.d4 followed by 2.Bf4

A "system" opening: White plays d4, Bf4, e3, Nf3, c3, Nbd2 and Bd3 in almost any order against almost anything. It is extremely popular with club players because there is little to memorise and the resulting positions are solid. The downside is that it can be passive; mix it with 1.e4 openings so you also learn open positions.

## Openings for Black

### Against 1.e4: the Sicilian Defence, 1...c5

The most combative reply. Black fights for d4 with a flank pawn rather than mirroring White, keeps the e-pawn to use later, and usually gets a half-open c-file for counterplay. After 2.Nf3 d6 3.d4 cxd4 4.Nxd4 Nf6 5.Nc3 the main lines begin. Expect sharp, unbalanced games.

### Against 1.e4: the French Defence, 1...e6

Black prepares ...d5 and challenges e4 next move. After 2.d4 d5 the centre locks up and the game becomes a battle of pawn chains, with Black attacking White's base with ...c5. The light-squared bishop on c8 can be a problem piece, so plan to free it or trade it.

### Against 1.e4: the Caro-Kann Defence, 1...c6

The same ...d5 idea, but with the c-pawn supporting it so the bishop on c8 can come out to f5 or g4 before ...e6. Very solid, with a reputation for giving Black good endgames. After 2.d4 d5 3.Nc3 dxe4 4.Nxe4 Bf5 is the classical line.

### Against 1.d4

The Queen's Gambit Declined (1...d5 2.c4 e6) is the simplest sound choice and mirrors what you will learn as White.

## Scholar's Mate and how to stop it

Sooner or later someone will try the four-move checkmate: 1.e4 e5 2.Qh5 Nc6 3.Bc4 Nf6?? 4.Qxf7#. The queen and bishop both aim at f7 and the knight on f6 blocked the one defender.

The defence is easy once you have seen it. After 2.Qh5, play 2...Nc6 to protect the e-pawn, and after 3.Bc4 play **3...g6**, attacking the queen. If 4.Qf3 (renewing the threat) then **4...Nf6** blocks it and Black has developed two pieces while White's queen has moved twice. Alternatively **3...Qe7** defends f7 directly. Either way, an early queen sortie should cost your opponent time, not win them the game.

## How the AI levels punish opening mistakes

Against **Buddy**, you can get away with almost anything, which makes it the perfect partner for rehearsing a new opening's first ten moves. **Nova** will sometimes grab a hanging pawn or trade off your best piece, so it keeps you honest without being ruthless. **Titan** notices when your king is still in the centre and when a piece is undefended, and it will open lines against an uncastled king. **Oracle** plays principled, deep moves from the first turn: if you fall behind in development it will convert the lead, so this is where good opening habits become essential rather than optional.

## Common mistakes

Grabbing pawns on the flank (like 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 Nf6 4.Nxe5?) before finishing development. Blocking your own bishops with pawns on their diagonals. Pushing the pawns in front of your castled king to chase a piece away. And playing an opening because its name sounds good rather than because you understand what it is for.

Centre pawns and knights first: the knights on f3 and c3 each attack two of the four central squares, which is why they come out before the bishops.

![Chess board after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 with the bishop on c4 highlighted and an arrow towards f7](/blog/chess-openings-italian.svg)

The Italian Game bishop points straight at f7, the square only the king defends; Black answers with 3...Bc5 or 3...Nf6 and keeps developing.

![Three mini boards showing kingside and queenside castling](/blog/chess-castling.svg)

Whichever opening you choose, the plan is the same: get the minor pieces out and castle so the king is safe before the fight begins.
