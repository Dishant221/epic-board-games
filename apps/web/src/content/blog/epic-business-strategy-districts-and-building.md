Epic Business looks like a dice game, and the dice do decide where you land. But over 45 minutes and a hundred rolls, the players who understand probability, cash flow and negotiation win far more often than the ones who just buy whatever they land on. This guide assumes you already know the rules (if not, start with [How to play Epic Business](/blog/how-to-play-epic-business)) and focuses on the decisions that actually move the needle.

## Know where the dice land

Two dice do not produce every total equally. There is one way to roll a 2 and one way to roll a 12, but six ways to roll a 7. Totals of 6, 7 and 8 together account for 16 of the 36 outcomes, or about 44% of all rolls. That has a direct consequence on the board.

Lock-up is the most visited corner in the game: players land on it, are sent there by cards and by Go to Lock-up, and roll three doubles into it. Every one of those players then leaves Lock-up and rolls two dice. So the districts **six to nine spaces after Lock-up** receive more traffic than any other stretch of the board. The colour groups on that side are the most valuable real estate per credit spent, and the station in the middle of that side is the best station.

The same logic applies in a weaker form to the spaces 6 to 8 after any card that moves many players to a fixed space. Watch where the Fortune deck tends to send people and buy just past it.

## Which districts to buy

### Buy early, buy often

In the first two laps, buy almost everything you land on. Property is scarce, there are only 22 districts, and anything you decline becomes a chance for a rival to complete a group at auction. The one exception is a lone district in a group where another player already holds two: it is a bargaining chip, not an asset, so bid on it only if you can trade it later.

### Target groups you can finish

A complete colour group is worth more than the sum of its districts because it unlocks building. Three-district groups are harder to complete than two-district groups, so the two-district groups near the start and finish of the lap are underrated: cheap to complete, cheap to build on, and they still double rent the moment you own both.

### Stations and utilities

Stations are solid, steady income with no building cost. Four stations produce a reliable rent on four of the forty spaces. Utilities are a weak investment on their own; buy them to deny a rival the pair, or when you have spare cash late on.

## When to build

### The three-office sweet spot

Rent does not grow in a straight line. On most districts the step from two to three offices is the largest single jump in the whole ladder, while the fourth office and the tower add comparatively less for their cost. Build to three offices across a group before you put a fourth anywhere. The exception is when a tower frees up a lot of cash relative to the extra rent, or when you have so much money that the question is moot.

### Keep a cash buffer

Never build down to zero. A sensible floor is enough to pay the highest rent you could plausibly land on in the next lap. Early in the game that might be 150 credits; once rivals have offices it is more like 500. Being forced to sell buildings at half price to pay a rent bill is the fastest way to lose a winning position.

### Build before the busy stretch

If your group sits just after Lock-up, build the moment you can afford it; traffic is coming. If your group is in the last stretch before Payday, you have more time, because players often take the card space or the tax before reaching you.

## Mortgages: a tool, not a failure

Mortgaging is how good players turn dead assets into buildings. A lone district that will never be completed earns almost nothing; mortgage it and spend the credits on a third office somewhere that matters. Only mortgage a district inside a group you intend to build on if you have no other option, because you cannot build on a group with a mortgaged member and the interest adds up.

Remember the order of operations when you are short: sell buildings on groups that are not generating rent first, mortgage loners second, and mortgage your money-making group last.

## Auction discipline

Auctions are the single biggest skill gap between new and experienced players. Three rules:

1. **Have a price in mind before bidding starts.** Decide what the property is worth to you (and what it is worth to deny a rival), then stop there.
2. **Bid on what you passed.** If you declined a property because you were saving cash, you can still pick it up cheaply if nobody else bids.
3. **Let the overbidder win.** When Buddy or a human bids 300 for a district priced at 120, say thank you and move on. Overpaying is how they lose.

## Trading

### With humans

Trades complete groups, and a trade that completes one group for you and one for them is usually good for both of you against the rest of the table. Offer cash sweeteners rather than giving away a district from a group you are close to finishing. Ask for the trade when the other player is cash-poor; they will value credits more highly than you do.

### With the AI

The four AI opponents evaluate trades differently. Buddy tends to accept almost anything that is not obviously terrible, so a lopsided deal often goes through. Nova wants a trade to look roughly even on face value. Titan tends to accept only trades that clearly improve its position, and the Oracle tends to accept only trades the maths says are good for it, which usually means a trade you should think twice about. If the Oracle agrees quickly, re-check what you just gave away.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying utilities on lap one** while declining a district that would have completed a group.
- **Building a tower on one district** instead of three offices on each district in the group.
- **Hoarding cash to "be safe"** while a rival builds; cash in hand earns nothing.
- **Trading away a station** as a throw-in. Stations are quiet earners for the whole game.
- **Ignoring the Lock-up side** because its districts look mid-priced. Traffic beats prestige.

## Quick reference

| Decision | Rule of thumb |
| --- | --- |
| Most-landed-on stretch | 6–9 spaces after Lock-up (7 is the most common roll) |
| Early game | Buy nearly everything; property is scarce |
| Best groups | Ones you can complete; two-district groups are underrated |
| Building order | Three offices on every district first, then a fourth or a tower |
| Cash buffer | At least the biggest rent you could pay in the next lap |
| Mortgage first | Loners outside any group you plan to build on |
| Auctions | Fix a maximum before bidding and stick to it |
| Trades with the Oracle | If it accepts instantly, re-check the maths |
