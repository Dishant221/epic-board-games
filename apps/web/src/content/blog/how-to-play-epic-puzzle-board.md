Not every game night needs a winner. Epic Puzzle Board is our cooperative jigsaw: choose one of more than twenty illustrations, pick how many pieces you want, and assemble it alone or with one other person who can be sitting next to you or on another continent. There is no timer unless you want one, no AI opponent, and no way to lose. This guide explains how the controls work, how the two-player mode shares a canvas, and the tried-and-tested jigsaw techniques that make a 200-piece picture come together in a quarter of an hour.

## What you need

A browser on any device. On a phone the pieces are larger and the tray scrolls with a swipe; on a tablet or laptop you see more of the canvas at once. For two players, you both need a free account so you can find each other in a private room. There is nothing to download.

## Setup

1. **Pick an image** from the gallery. The collection includes paintings, cartoons and mythical creatures, and the thumbnails show you what you will be building.
2. **Pick a piece count.** Smaller counts are ideal for children and for a quick break; larger counts are for a long evening or a two-person session.
3. **Choose solo or two-player.** In two-player mode, create a private room and invite a friend, or ping someone from your Friends list.

The canvas appears with a faint outline of the finished picture, and the pieces are shuffled into the tray along the bottom of the screen.

## How it works

### The tray

All loose pieces live in the **scrolling tray** at the bottom. Swipe or use the arrows to scroll through it. Pieces are shown at a reduced size in the tray so you can see many at once; they grow to full size when you pick one up.

### Dragging and snapping

Press and hold a piece in the tray, drag it onto the canvas and let go. If you drop it **close to its correct position**, it snaps into place with a satisfying click and locks there. If you drop it somewhere else, it stays where you left it on the canvas so you can use the canvas as a sorting area. You can always drag a loose piece back to the tray.

### Two players, one canvas

With two players you both see the same canvas and the same tray, and **either of you can place any piece at any time**. There are no turns. Snapped pieces lock for both of you, and you see each other's drags in real time, so you can hand a piece over by dropping it near where your partner is working.

### Finishing

When the last piece snaps, the outline disappears, the full illustration is revealed with a small celebration, and you are offered another picture. That is the whole game: complete the picture to win, together.

## All the rules, in brief

- Pick an image from the gallery and a piece count.
- Scroll the piece tray at the bottom and drag pieces onto the canvas.
- Pieces snap into place when they are close to the correct spot.
- With two players you solve together; both of you can place pieces at any time.
- Complete the picture to finish.

## Technique: how experienced puzzlers work

### Corners, then edges

Every rectangular jigsaw has exactly four corner pieces (two flat sides) and a ring of edge pieces (one flat side). Scroll through the tray once, pulling every flat-sided piece onto the canvas as you go. Build the frame first; it defines the space and gives every later piece a reference point.

### Sort by colour and texture

Once the frame is done, scan the tray again and group pieces by dominant colour: sky, foreground, a character's cloak. Park each group in a corner of the canvas. You are not placing anything yet; you are reducing the number of candidates for each hole.

### Work from the frame inwards

Pick the most distinctive region of the picture (a face, a lettered sign, a bright object) and complete it, then connect it to the frame. Large flat areas such as sky come last, when there are few pieces left and shape alone can tell you where each one goes.

### Use the snap as a hint

Because pieces snap when they are close, you can "test" a piece by dropping it where you think it belongs. If it does not snap, it does not belong there. Resist the urge to do this with every piece, though; it is slower than looking properly.

## Co-op etiquette

- **Split the work.** One person builds the frame while the other sorts colours, or divide the picture top and bottom.
- **Talk about regions, not pieces.** "I'll do the castle, you take the sky" works better than describing individual shapes.
- **Don't fight over a hole.** If you both reach for the same gap, the snap goes to whoever gets there first; laugh and move on.
- **Leave the last piece for the newest player.** It is a small gesture that makes children (and grandparents) beam.

## Playing with a child or a grandparent

Pick a low piece count and a bright cartoon, switch on voice chat so you can talk as you work, and let the less experienced player do the edges first; the flat sides make them easy to spot and placing them feels like real progress. The canvas is forgiving: nothing can be placed wrongly, so there is no way to make a mistake that has to be undone. For a grandparent on a tablet, a landscape orientation gives the biggest canvas.

## Is there an AI?

No. Epic Puzzle Board has no computer opponent and no hint button, by design. The game is about the picture and the company. If you want a relaxed solo puzzle *with* hints, try [Mahjong Solitaire](/games/mahjong) or [Sudoku](/games/sudoku).

## Common mistakes

- **Scrolling the tray endlessly looking for one piece.** Pull candidates onto the canvas instead and sort them there.
- **Starting in the middle.** Without the frame you have no anchors and every piece has too many possible homes.
- **Dropping pieces exactly on top of each other on the canvas.** Spread your sorting piles out so you can see every piece.
- **Choosing too many pieces for the screen you are on.** On a phone, a smaller count is more fun than a crowded tray.
