Tic Tac Toe (noughts and crosses, if you grew up calling it that) is probably the first strategy game most of us ever learned. Its rules fit on a napkin, a round takes two minutes, and yet it quietly teaches the same ideas that run through chess and Connect 4: threats, blocking and forcing your opponent to choose between two bad options.

This guide covers the rules as our version plays them, the vocabulary you need to talk about the board, the reason almost every game between careful players ends in a draw, and how to make it a fun game for the youngest players in the house.

## What you need

Two players and a 3×3 grid. Online, that is one friend, one stranger from matchmaking or one of our AI opponents. Rounds are so quick that the game shines as a series: first to five wins, swapping who plays X each round.

## Setup

The board is a grid of nine empty squares arranged three by three. One player is **X** and the other is **O**. In our game, as in the classic, **X always moves first**. When you play a series, swap symbols between rounds so the first-move advantage is shared.

### Naming the squares

![Tic Tac Toe grid with centre, corner and edge squares labelled](/blog/ttt-squares.svg)

There are only three kinds of square, and the names matter because the strategy is built on them:

- The **centre** sits on four winning lines: the middle row, the middle column and both diagonals.
- Each of the four **corners** sits on three lines: a row, a column and one diagonal.
- Each of the four **edges** (the middle squares of each side) sits on only two lines: a row and a column.

Four plus four plus one is nine squares, and there are exactly eight winning lines in total.

## How a turn works

On your turn, tap or click any empty square to place your symbol. That is the whole turn. The game checks for three in a row after every move; if you have made a line, you win immediately and the line is highlighted. Otherwise your opponent moves. There is no passing.

## All the rules

1. **X moves first.** Then players alternate.
2. **Mark an empty square on your turn.** You cannot move or remove a symbol once placed.
3. **Three in a row wins.** The line can run across a row, down a column or along either diagonal.
4. **Nine filled squares with no line is a draw.** Because X moves first, X places five symbols and O places four in a full-board game.

## Why the game is usually a draw

Tic Tac Toe is small enough that every possible game has been counted. There are **255,168** different ways a game can be played out when you count every sequence of moves, and only **765** essentially different positions once you ignore rotations and reflections. Analysts worked through all of them long ago, and the conclusion is simple: if neither player makes a mistake, the game is always a draw.

That does not make the game pointless. It means the game is really about *mistakes*: spotting the moments when your opponent gives you a chance, and refusing to give them one. For children, that is a wonderful introduction to looking one move ahead.

## Openings and basic strategy

### As X (first player)

The strongest opening is a **corner**. It threatens nothing yet, but it leaves O exactly one safe reply: the centre. Any other reply loses to a fork (see below). The centre is also a fine opening and is easier to understand for beginners; an edge opening is the weakest, because edges touch the fewest lines.

### As O (second player)

- If X took a corner, **take the centre**. Nothing else holds.
- If X took the centre, **take a corner**. An edge reply loses.
- If X took an edge, take the centre, or either adjacent corner; all of these are safe.

### Forks: the only way to win against someone who blocks

![A Tic Tac Toe position where X threatens two lines at once](/blog/ttt-fork.svg)

Once both players block single threats, the only way to win is to create **two threats at the same time**. That is called a fork. Your opponent can block only one line, so you complete the other. In the diagram, X has corners top-left and bottom-right, O has made the mistake of answering with a corner instead of blocking a line, and X's third corner creates a fork along the left column and the bottom row.

Most Tic Tac Toe wins at any age come from a fork the loser did not see forming.

## Common mistakes

- **Opening on an edge.** Edges sit on only two lines and give your opponent an easy game.
- **Answering a corner opening with the opposite corner.** It looks symmetrical and clever; it loses to a fork.
- **Blocking instead of winning.** Always check whether *you* can complete a line before you block theirs. It sounds obvious and it happens constantly.
- **Playing the diagonal reflex.** Taking a corner just because it is on a diagonal, without checking whether it gives your opponent a fork square.

## Playing with friends and family

Tic Tac Toe is a brilliant family game precisely because it is tiny. Children as young as four can play; the rules take one demonstration and there is no reading involved. A few ideas:

- **Play a series and swap symbols.** First to five, X alternates each round.
- **Let the youngest player explain their move.** "I went there to stop you" is the first strategic sentence most children ever say.
- **Try "blind" rounds** where you name squares aloud and the other person has to picture the board.

Online, pick **Online Multiplayer** for a quick opponent via matchmaking, or create a **private room** and ping a friend or family member from your friends list. Text and voice chat work during the game, and a two-minute round is the perfect length for a chat while you play.

## Choosing an AI level

![The four AI levels, from Buddy to Oracle](/blog/ai-levels.svg)

- **Buddy** plays a random move most of the time. Children can beat Buddy, and the wins are real: Buddy will sometimes miss an open three, so the child learns to look for it.
- **Nova** blocks and wins more often than not, but still slips. A good first step up.
- **Titan** rarely makes a mistake. Beating Titan usually needs a corner opening and a fork.
- **Oracle** never makes a mistake. Because perfect play always draws, **you cannot beat the Oracle**; the best possible result is a draw, and achieving it every time is a genuine milestone. Our guide to [drawing with the Oracle](/blog/how-to-beat-the-oracle-at-tic-tac-toe) shows exactly how.
