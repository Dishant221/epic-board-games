Checkers, or draughts if you grew up in the UK, is the game many of us learned before chess and then quietly forgot. It deserves a second look. The rules take two minutes, yet the mandatory-capture rule gives every position a hidden tactical edge, and a well-played game between two people who know what they are doing is tense right to the last piece. Here is everything you need to play on Epic Board Games.

## What you need

Two players and a browser. On Epic Board Games you can play a friend in a private room, be matched with another player online, or take on one of four AI opponents. Legal moves are highlighted when you select a piece, forced captures are enforced automatically, and multi-jumps let you keep tapping through the sequence.

## Setup

The board is the same 8×8 grid used for chess, but only the 32 dark squares are ever used. Each player places 12 pieces (also called men or checkers) on the dark squares of the three rows nearest them, leaving the two middle rows empty. Orient the board so that each player has a dark square in their bottom-left corner; our board does this for you and rotates so your pieces are always at the bottom.

In the version played here, one side has red pieces and the other dark pieces. In the standard rules of English draughts and American checkers the darker colour moves first, and that is the convention most players will recognise; whichever colour you are given, the turn indicator makes it clear who is to move.

## How a turn works

On your turn you make exactly one move with one piece. A move is either a simple step or a capture, and if a capture is available you must take one. After you move, your opponent does the same. Play continues until someone has no pieces left or no legal move.

## All the rules

### Simple moves

An ordinary piece moves one square diagonally forward onto an empty dark square. "Forward" means towards the opponent's side of the board. Pieces never move sideways or straight ahead, and an uncrowned piece never moves backwards.

### Captures

You capture by jumping: if an enemy piece sits diagonally in front of you and the square directly beyond it is empty, you leap over it to that empty square and the jumped piece is removed. You cannot jump your own pieces, and you cannot jump two enemy pieces in one hop; there must be exactly one piece between your starting and landing squares.

### Captures are mandatory

This is the rule that gives checkers its character. If any of your pieces can capture, you must capture with one of them; you may not make a quiet move instead. If more than one capture is available you may choose which to take, but you cannot decline. Our game enforces this automatically: when a capture is on the board, only capturing moves are offered.

### Multi-jumps

After landing from a jump, if the same piece can jump again, it must keep going, taking as many pieces as the chain allows. A piece may change direction between hops (always diagonally), but it must not jump the same piece twice. A single turn can sweep three or four enemy pieces off the board.

### Kings

A piece that reaches the far row (your opponent's back rank) is crowned a king and in a physical set would have a second checker stacked on top; on our board a king shows a crown mark. A king moves and captures exactly like an ordinary piece but in all four diagonal directions, so it can go backwards as well as forwards. If a piece reaches the back rank in the middle of a multi-jump, in the standard rules the turn ends there: the new king does not continue jumping until a later turn.

In the version played on Epic Board Games kings move one square at a time, as in English draughts and American checkers, rather than the "flying kings" of international draughts that slide any distance along a diagonal.

### Winning

You win when your opponent has no pieces left, or when it is their turn and they have no legal move (every remaining piece is blocked). That second condition catches people out: a blocked player loses, they do not pass. In the standard rules a game can also be agreed drawn when neither side can make progress, for instance one king chasing another around the board.

## Your first moves

Early on, aim to advance from the centre rather than the edges, keep your pieces in supporting pairs so that a jump against one lands on a square where it can be recaptured, and try not to leave a piece on its own in front of your position. Before every move ask one question: if I go here, what captures does that force or allow? Because captures are compulsory, you can often work out exactly what your opponent will do next.

## Common beginner mistakes

Rushing every piece forward and abandoning the back rank, which hands your opponent easy kings. Making a capture without checking whether it drags your piece onto a square where it gets taken back twice. Trading pieces when you are behind, which simply speeds up the loss. And forgetting that a king can move backwards, so a piece "safely" behind it is not safe at all.

Checkers is a brilliant family game because a seven-year-old can genuinely beat an adult who is not concentrating. ## Choosing an AI level

![The four AI levels, Buddy, Nova, Titan and Oracle, arranged from relaxed to perfect play](/blog/ai-levels.svg)

- **Buddy** mostly moves at random, so it will hand you pieces and let you practise multi-jumps and kinging.
- **Nova** plays sensibly most of the time but drops a piece now and again. Good for learning what a "safe" move feels like.
- **Titan** will set up forced exchanges and punish a broken back rank. A real test of your reading.
- **Oracle** never makes a deliberate mistake and searches deeply within its time budget. Checkers is a game of forced sequences, so expect to be out-calculated unless you play very tidily.
