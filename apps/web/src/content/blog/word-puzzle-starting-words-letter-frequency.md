Your first guess in Word Puzzle is the only one you make with zero information, so it is the one most worth planning. A good opener is not about luck; it is about asking the question that eliminates the most possibilities whatever the answer turns out to be. This guide explains the letter statistics behind strong starting words, compares the popular choices, and gives you a simple two-opener plan that turns most rounds into a four-guess finish. If you need the rules first, start with [How to play Word Puzzle](/blog/how-to-play-word-puzzle).

## Letter frequency in English

Count the letters in a large pile of English text and a familiar order appears. Approximate shares, from standard frequency tables:

| Letter | E | T | A | O | I | N | S | H | R | D | L |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| % | 12.7 | 9.1 | 8.2 | 7.5 | 7.0 | 6.7 | 6.3 | 6.1 | 6.0 | 4.3 | 4.0 |

The top nine letters, often remembered as ETAOIN SHR, account for roughly 70% of all letters in running text. A guess built from them is almost guaranteed to light up a few tiles.

## Why five-letter words are different

Running text is dominated by short function words ("the", "of", "and"), which inflate T, H and N. Five-letter dictionary words have their own profile. In most five-letter word lists, **S, E, A, O, R, I, L, T and N** are the dominant letters, with S pushed up by plurals and verb forms and H pushed down. That is why the best openers look like SLATE or CRANE rather than THINE. Vowels also matter more in a short word: with only five slots, finding two vowels early tells you a great deal about the shape of the answer.

## The popular openers compared

- **CRANE** — C, R, A, N, E. Two very common consonants, two vowels and the near-universal E in final position. Widely regarded as one of the best all-round openers.
- **SLATE** — S, L, A, T, E. Tests the plural-friendly S in position 1 and the common -ATE ending. Pairs beautifully with a second word that covers R, N and O.
- **TRACE** — T, R, A, C, E. Nearly identical coverage to CRANE with T swapped for N; choose whichever you find easier to remember.
- **STARE** — S, T, A, R, E. Five top-tier letters; the only cost is that S, T and R are so common that a yellow can be hard to place.
- **ADIEU / AUDIO** — four vowels in one go. You learn the vowel structure immediately, but at the price of weak consonant coverage. Good if you enjoy a quick picture of the word's skeleton, less efficient overall.
- **Vowel-heavy consonant blends such as ROATE or RAISE** — RAISE is a real word and a fine opener; ROATE-type words are accepted in some lists and rejected in others, so check before you rely on one.

There is no single perfect word. Any opener with four or five distinct common letters, at least two of them vowels, and no repeats, is good enough to win most rounds.

## Positions matter too

Frequency by position changes the picture again. Typical patterns across five-letter word lists:

- **Position 1:** S is by far the most common start, followed by C, B, T and P.
- **Position 2:** vowels dominate — A, O, R, E, I.
- **Position 3:** more vowels — A, I, O, E, U.
- **Position 4:** E, N, S, A and L.
- **Position 5:** E and Y lead, then T, S and R.

This is why E in the fifth slot and S in the first slot are such good bets, and why a yellow E usually wants to move to position 5 or 4. It also explains the value of Y: it is rare overall but common as a final letter, so if your vowels have come up empty, think of words ending in -Y.

## Common endings and patterns

A quick checklist when you have the first two or three letters:

- **-ER, -ED, -ES, -EN, -EL** cover a huge range of words.
- **-LY, -TY, -RY, -NY** account for most words ending in Y.
- **-ST, -NT, -CH, -SH, -TH** are frequent consonant endings.
- Double letters (LL, SS, EE, OO, TT) appear in a meaningful minority of answers; consider them when the colours stop making sense.

## Second-guess strategy: new letters or exploit the greens?

After your opener you face a real choice. One approach is to **explore**: play a second word made entirely of letters you have not yet tested, ignoring the greens and yellows, to maximise information. The other is to **exploit**: build on what you found immediately.

A reliable rule of thumb: if your opener gave you **zero or one** coloured tile, explore. If it gave you **two or more**, start exploiting, but still try to include at least two new letters. Exploring too long leaves you with knowledge but no guesses; exploiting too early leaves you guessing among many candidates that share a pattern, such as SHARE, SHAKE, SHAME and SHAPE.

## The two-opener plan

A simple plan that many strong players use: memorise a pair of words with ten different letters that together cover the most common ones, and always play them first unless the opener already produced three or more greens. Good pairs include **CRANE + SLOTH**, **SLATE + CRONY** and **TRACE + SOLID**. After two guesses you have tested ten letters, including every top-tier vowel and consonant, and the keyboard has usually narrowed the answer to a handful of words. Your third guess is then a proper deduction rather than a hope.

## Common mistakes

- **Repeating a letter in the opener.** SPEED tests only four letters; SPEND tests five.
- **Chasing a yellow into the wrong slot.** If R was yellow in position 2, it is not in position 2. Move it.
- **Forgetting the keyboard.** Grey letters are gone; do not spend tiles on them.
- **Ignoring position statistics.** A yellow S almost always belongs at the front; a yellow E almost always belongs at the end.
- **Abandoning the plan after one bad opener.** A grey-heavy first row is still five eliminations; the plan is working.

## Quick reference

| Question | Short answer |
| --- | --- |
| Best letters to test first | E, A, R, O, T, L, S, N, I |
| Strong single openers | CRANE, SLATE, TRACE, STARE, RAISE |
| Strong pairs | CRANE + SLOTH, SLATE + CRONY, TRACE + SOLID |
| Most common first letter | S |
| Most common last letters | E, Y |
| When to explore | 0–1 coloured tiles after the opener |
| When to exploit | 2+ coloured tiles |
